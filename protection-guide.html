A practical family protection guide

Know what a policy can do, what it cannot do, and which questions to ask before you buy. A worksheet-first approach to protecting the people who depend on you.

Published by TrueVine Financial. Editorial standards and corrections.

1. Start with the household, not an income multiplier

A rule such as "buy ten times your salary" skips important differences between families. The same income can support one adult or a household with children, debt, and unpaid caregiving. Start by asking which expenses would continue, which would disappear, and which new costs would arise if a family member died.

One discussion framework is: temporary support needs + one-time obligations + future goals - resources genuinely available = a starting protection gap. Separate money your family can actually access from retirement balances, home equity, or assets you do not intend to sell.

The support calculation is $2,500 × 12 × 8 = $240,000. It does not model inflation, investment returns, taxes, benefit eligibility, or changing expenses. Eight years is an example, not a standard. If a mortgage will be paid off from a lump sum, do not also count those same mortgage payments in the monthly shortfall. Count new childcare costs only once, too.

Your next step: make two versions of the worksheet: one using the household as it is today, and one using a realistic higher-cost scenario. Record your assumptions so a professional can challenge them instead of treating the result as a precise answer.

2. Compare term and permanent life insurance by purpose

Life insurance generally pays a death benefit to designated beneficiaries after a covered death. It does not remove every financial risk. Coverage depends on the contract being in force and the claim satisfying its terms. Read the actual policy, not only a brochure or an illustration.

Ask for two separate explanations

First ask what is guaranteed, including the conditions required to maintain that guarantee. Then ask what is merely illustrated. A projected value is not a promise. Ask what happens if you stop paying, reduce payments, borrow, or surrender the policy. Loans can reduce available benefits and may create tax consequences if a policy lapses or is surrendered.

For employer-provided life insurance, check the amount, whether it changes with employment, and any portability or conversion deadlines. Do not assume coverage follows you automatically. If replacing a policy, understand new underwriting, surrender charges, and applicable contestability provisions before canceling existing protection.

3. Living benefits are not a general hardship fund

"Living benefits" is a broad marketing phrase. An accelerated death benefit rider may let an insured access part of a death benefit following a qualifying terminal, chronic, or critical illness, but not every rider covers every category. A diagnosis alone may not satisfy a contract's definition. Ordinary unemployment, a difficult month, or an uncovered injury does not automatically qualify.

Before treating a rider as part of a plan, ask for the rider itself and find:

  • The exact covered condition, medical evidence, and any waiting or survival period.
  • The maximum benefit, minimum remaining death benefit, fees, discounts, or other adjustments.
  • How a payment changes premiums, cash value, loans, and the amount beneficiaries may later receive.
  • Whether receiving money could affect taxes or means-tested public benefits in your circumstances.

For a simplified arithmetic example, if a $300,000 death benefit is reduced dollar-for-dollar by a $60,000 acceleration, $240,000 remains before other adjustments. Actual riders may use discounts, charges, or different reduction formulas, so the check you receive and the reduction may not match.

Do not confuse this with disability income insurance, which may replace part of earned income when a contractual definition of disability is met, or with comprehensive long-term care coverage. Compare the triggering events, not just the labels.

4. Map the first 90 days of an income interruption

A death-benefit plan and an income-interruption plan solve different problems. Build a timeline showing when pay stops, what resources are available, and when any benefits could begin. Include utilities, housing, groceries, debt minimums, care costs, and insurance premiums.

Suppose essential expenses are $3,600 a month and available emergency cash is $7,200. That covers about two months before new expenses or other income are considered. A hypothetical disability policy with a 90-day elimination period would not bridge that entire waiting period. A $1,800 monthly benefit would also leave an ongoing $1,800 monthly expense gap in this simplified example.

  1. Days 1-30: identify paid leave, accessible cash, and bills that cannot wait.
  2. Days 31-60: check employer benefit eligibility, claim requirements, and any reduced income.
  3. Days 61-90 and beyond: model remaining savings and the amount and duration of benefits that actually apply.

For a disability policy, ask about the definition of disability, exclusions, elimination period, benefit period, income limits, offsets, and tax treatment. Do not count a benefit until you understand its eligibility rules. A self-employed person may also need to distinguish personal living expenses from business overhead. The CFPB's budgeting and cash-flow tools can help organize the numbers before a coverage discussion.

5. Make your estate plan usable by someone else

Estate planning is not only deciding an inheritance amount. It also means making it possible for someone you trust to locate documents and understand your wishes during a stressful period. Keep an inventory of accounts, policies, professional contacts, and document locations. Do not put passwords in an ordinary shared email or leave sensitive documents publicly accessible.

  • Beneficiaries: review primary and contingent designations after marriage, divorce, a birth, or a death. Naming a minor can require special planning; ask an attorney about appropriate arrangements.
  • Legal documents: discuss wills, financial powers of attorney, health-care directives, and guardianship questions with an attorney licensed in the relevant state.
  • Access: tell a trusted person where documents are held and how to contact the professionals involved.
  • Consistency: ask how ownership, beneficiary designations, and estate documents interact. Do not assume a will automatically overrides every beneficiary designation.

A useful test is: "Could my trusted person find the right insurer and policy number within an hour?" If not, improving the document inventory may be the most useful first step. Review the inventory when circumstances change, not just when purchasing a new product.

6. Separate business continuity from personal protection

A business owner can have three different gaps: money the household needs, expenses the business must keep paying, and the ownership transition if an owner dies or cannot work. A personal life policy does not by itself decide who can sign checks, serve clients, or buy an ownership share.

Consider a two-owner service business. If one owner becomes unavailable, who can access records, contact clients, authorize payroll, and supervise work next week? A continuity checklist should name a backup person, record authority, explain access controls, and include an up-to-date contact list.

Key-person coverage, business overhead expense coverage, and funding for a buy-sell agreement serve different purposes. An attorney should help structure an ownership agreement; a tax professional should review ownership and beneficiary arrangements. Insurance funding must match the actual agreement and its valuation process, rather than assuming any policy solves the transition.

Use the SBA's emergency-preparedness resources alongside an insurance review. Coverage is one tool, not a substitute for a written operational plan.

Questions to bring to your next conversation

How much life insurance is enough?

There is no universal number. Estimate the expenses and goals your household would need to fund, subtract resources actually available, and test different time periods and costs. The worked example above is a framework for questions, not a quote or a recommendation.

Does life insurance cover every illness or loss of income?

No. A life policy generally pays for a covered death. Access during life depends on specific policy provisions, and disability income coverage has separate definitions and requirements. Ask for written terms rather than relying on a phrase such as "full protection."

Should I cancel an old policy when requesting a new quote?

Requesting a quote does not establish coverage. Check replacement costs, guarantees, underwriting, and the new policy's effective date before deciding what to do with existing coverage. Ask an appropriately licensed professional to explain the consequences in writing.

How do I check an insurance professional?

Ask for the professional's legal name, license number, authorized states, and insurers represented. Verify these with your state insurance department. Ask how the professional is paid and whether the products discussed are limited to particular companies.
